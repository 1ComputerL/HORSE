Horse Nutrition & Diet

Understanding proper equine nutrition for optimal health and performance

Natural Diet of Horses

In the wild, horses are continuous grazers, spending 14-18 hours per day eating. Their digestive system is specifically adapted for this constant intake of plant material.

Wild Grazing Habits

  • Horses graze on grasses, clover, and legumes
  • They travel several miles daily while foraging
  • Diet changes seasonally based on available vegetation
  • They prefer young, tender grass over mature plants
  • Water sources are essential and accessed multiple times daily

Domestic Horse Nutrition Requirements

Daily Caloric Needs

A 1,000 lb (454 kg) horse at maintenance (light activity) requires approximately 15,000-20,000 calories per day. Requirements increase significantly with exercise.

Activity Level Caloric Need per 1,000 lbs Percentage Increase
Maintenance (light activity) 15,000-20,000 cal/day Baseline
Light work (1 hour/day) 20,000-25,000 cal/day +25-33%
Moderate work (2-3 hours/day) 25,000-30,000 cal/day +33-50%
Heavy work/competition 30,000-40,000 cal/day +50-100%
Endurance/racing 40,000-60,000 cal/day +100-200%

Primary Feedstuffs

Hay & Pasture (50-100% of diet)

  • Timothy Hay: Most common, good nutritional balance
  • Alfalfa: Higher protein and calcium, often mixed with timothy
  • Orchard Grass: Palatable, nutritious
  • Fresh Pasture: Ideal when available, requires 1-2 acres minimum per horse
  • Minimum Daily Amount: 1.5-2% of body weight (15-20 lbs for 1,000 lb horse)
Quality Matters:

Hay quality is determined by color (bright green is best), aroma (should smell fresh, not musty), and stage of maturity. Poor quality hay should not be used as it lacks nutrients and may cause digestive issues.

Grains & Concentrates

  • Oats: Safest grain, easily digestible, traditional choice
  • Barley: Higher energy, starchier than oats
  • Corn: High energy but must be carefully balanced
  • Commercial Grain Mixes: Formulated with proper vitamin/mineral ratios
  • Quantity: Should not exceed 50% of total feed by weight

Supplements & Treats

  • Vegetables (carrots, apples) - good treats in moderation
  • Mineral blocks - provide salt and trace minerals
  • Joint supplements for older horses or those in hard work
  • Probiotics and enzymes - aid digestion
  • Vitamin supplements - sometimes necessary depending on diet

Essential Nutrients

Nutrient Function Source
Protein Muscle development and repair Hay, alfalfa, grains
Fiber Digestive health Hay, pasture
Carbohydrates Energy Grains, hay
Fats Energy, coat health Oils, grains
Calcium Bone health Alfalfa, supplements
Phosphorus Bone and energy Grains, hay
Magnesium Muscle function Hay, supplements
Iron Blood health Most feeds
Zinc Immune function, hoof health Supplements, grains
Copper Hair/hoof pigmentation Supplements, hay

Water Requirements

Water is the most essential nutrient. Horses require:

  • Maintenance: 5-10 gallons per day
  • During Exercise: 10-15+ gallons per day
  • Hot Weather: 20+ gallons per day
  • Winter: Heated water encourages drinking
  • Constant Access: Fresh, clean water should always be available
Important: Dehydration is serious and can lead to colic, poor performance, and health problems. Never restrict water intake except under veterinary guidance after strenuous exercise.

Feeding Best Practices

  • Feed Small Portions Frequently: 3-4 meals daily mimics natural grazing
  • Consistency: Keep feeding times and amounts consistent
  • Quality Over Quantity: Feed good quality forage rather than excessive grain
  • Gradual Changes: Change feed slowly over 2-3 weeks to avoid digestive upset
  • Monitor Weight: Adjust feed based on body condition score
  • Individual Needs: Young, old, and hard-working horses have different requirements
  • Avoid Moldy Feed: Moldy hay or grain can cause serious illness

Special Considerations

Young Horses (Foals & Weanlings)

  • Require higher protein and calories for growth
  • Need balanced calcium and phosphorus ratios
  • Should be fed high-quality grain formulated for growth
  • Gradual introduction to solid feed starting at 6-8 weeks

Senior Horses (15+ years)

  • Dental problems may require softer feed or soaking
  • May have lower nutrient absorption
  • Often need higher protein and calories despite lower activity
  • More susceptible to nutritional deficiencies

Horses in Hard Work

  • Dramatically increased caloric needs
  • Increased electrolyte requirements
  • More frequent, smaller meals essential
  • Timing of meals relative to exercise is important

Common Nutritional Disorders

  • Colic: Often related to poor feeding practices or sudden feed changes
  • Laminitis: Can be triggered by excessive grain or rich pasture
  • Nutritional Imbalances: Improper mineral ratios can cause bone and joint problems
  • Developmental Orthopedic Disease: Young horses overfed with high energy diets
Consult a Professional:

Work with an equine nutritionist or veterinarian to develop a feeding plan specific to your horse's age, activity level, and health status. Proper nutrition is an investment in your horse's long-term health.
